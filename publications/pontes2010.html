Abstract

This work addresses the linear stability of the classical hydrodynamic solution in the vicinity of a large rotating disk, modified by coupling through viscosity to the concentration field of a chemical species. The configuration corresponds to electrochemical cells with iron rotating disk electrodes that dissolve in a 1 M H2SO4 electrolyte solution. The polarization curves measured in these cells exhibit an instability at the beginning of the region in which the current is controlled by hydrodynamic transport. The instability appears at a particular applied potential and is suppressed above a higher potential. Electrode dissolution produces a concentration boundary layer whose thickness is approximately 4% of that of the hydrodynamic boundary layer. The concentration boundary layer increases the interfacial solution viscosity and decreases the diffusion coefficient in the electrolyte, coupling the two fields and affecting the hydrodynamics. Because the current is proportional to the interfacial concentration gradient of the ion-carrying chemical species, instability of the coupled fields may explain the experimentally observed current instability. This work discusses studies conducted by the Department and Graduate Program in Metallurgical and Materials Engineering at the Federal University of Rio de Janeiro, together with the Mechanical Engineering Graduate Program and the Group for Environmental Studies and Simulations in Reservoirs at the State University of Rio de Janeiro. The results show that increases of approximately 2% in interfacial viscosity significantly reduce the stability of modes found in constant-viscosity configurations. Further increases in interfacial viscosity lead to a new unstable region at Reynolds numbers far below the stability threshold of the previously known region. Concentration oscillations of unstable modes in the new region have much greater amplitudes than the corresponding hydrodynamic oscillations. These concentration modes are confined to a boundary layer much thinner than the hydrodynamic one, resulting in interfacial concentration-gradient oscillations that appear large enough to produce the current oscillations observed experimentally. Numerical results show that progressively increasing interfacial viscosity initially reduces flow stability, but beyond a certain threshold the stability properties of constant-viscosity fluids are recovered. This suggests that the mechanism may account for the suppression of current oscillations when the applied potential exceeds a certain value. Solutions and initial results obtained with a direct numerical simulation code developed by our group are also discussed.
